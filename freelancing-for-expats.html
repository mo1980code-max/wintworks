Freelancing Abroad 2026: The Expat's Guide to Visas, Taxes & Getting Paid

Reviewed by WintWorks Editorial Team · Last updated September 11, 2026 · 10 min read

Freelancing is the shortest legal bridge between "I've moved" and "I have income": no single employer has to sponsor you, and skills like design, development, writing, translation and marketing sell across borders. But doing it legally means three things are true at once — you hold the right visa, you pay the right taxes, and you can actually get paid across currencies. This guide walks through all three for Europe and the US market.

Rule one: your visa decides if you may freelance at all

  • Tourist / short-stay visas allow no work — including remote work for clients back home. "I'm just working on my laptop" is still work in the eyes of immigration authorities.
  • Employment visas usually tie you to one employer. Side clients can violate your permit's conditions — check before you take them.
  • Student visas are usually restrictive. Some countries let students freelance within their annual work allowance (Germany's 140-day rule can cover freelance work); others ban self-employment outright (the UK Student visa does). See the student visa work guide.
  • The fix is a freelance / self-employment / digital-nomad permit — every route below exists for exactly this purpose.

Visa routes that let you freelance (2026 overview)

🇩🇪 Germany — Freelance visa

For recognised liberal professions (Freiberufler: IT, design, writing, consulting, teaching…). Show a viable business: clients or letters of intent, funds and health insurance. A proven path to settlement.

🇳🇱 Netherlands — Self-employment

Points-based assessment (RVO) scoring your education, experience, business plan and contribution to the Dutch economy. US citizens have the faster DAFT treaty route with a modest registered capital deposit.

🇪🇸 Spain — Digital nomad / autónomo

Visa for remote employees and freelancers with a work contract or steady clients, income at a multiple of the Spanish minimum wage and relevant qualifications or experience. Register as autónomo to invoice locally.

🇵🇹 Portugal — D8 digital nomad

For remote workers and freelancers with average income of about four times the local minimum wage over recent months. Popular with Lisbon and Porto's expat scene; leads to residence and citizenship options.

🇪🇪 Estonia — DN visa + e-Residency

Digital nomad visa requires proof of a high monthly income over recent months (roughly €4,500/month under recent rules). Pair it with e-Residency to run an EU company fully online.

🇨🇿 Czechia — Trade licence (živno)

A cheap, fast trade licence plus long-stay visa made Prague a freelancer hub. Requires proof of accommodation, funds and the licence itself; income and tax records follow Czech rules.

🇬🇷🇭🇷🇷🇴 Also consider

Greece, Croatia and Romania run digital-nomad permits, and most EU states have some self-employment route. Income thresholds and paperwork vary — always check the official immigration page.

🇺🇸 USA — the contractor angle

No freelance visa exists for the US. Non-Americans usually work for US clients as contractors from their own country, or through an employer-of-record that handles sponsorship. B-1 visits still ban productive work.

Income thresholds and documents change every year — treat the numbers above as orientation and confirm on the official immigration site before you apply. If you'd rather take a salaried job first, compare Europe's work visa routes.

Remote employee vs freelancer — pick the right legal box

  • Freelancer / contractor: you invoice clients, handle your own tax and social security, and control your time. Highest freedom, most admin.
  • Remote employee abroad: the employer needs a legal entity or an employer-of-record (EOR) in your country. You get payslips and social contributions; less freedom over hours.
  • Warning sign: a "freelance contract" where one company controls your hours, tools and holidays may be ruled fake self-employment (misclassification) — both you and the client can owe back taxes.

Taxes: the 183-day rule and what it really means

  • Tax residency usually follows where your life is centred — staying more than ~183 days in a country in a year is the common trigger, but "centre of vital interests" can catch you earlier.
  • Double-taxation treaties decide which country taxes which income when two states claim you. Most treaties use a tie-breaker: permanent home → centre of life → habitual abode → nationality.
  • US citizens file US taxes worldwide no matter where they live (with exclusions and credits that often reduce the bill to zero — but the filing duty remains).
  • EU B2B services usually use the reverse-charge mechanism: you invoice without VAT, the business client accounts for it at home. Register for VAT once you cross your country's thresholds or sell to consumers across the EU.
  • Keep every invoice and contract for years — they are also your income proof for visa renewals and citizenship applications.

Tax law is country-specific and this guide is general information, not advice — budget for one session with a local tax advisor when you set up. It usually pays for itself.

Social security, health insurance and pensions

  • Self-employed expats generally register in the country where they work and pay local contributions (Germany's public health insurance for freelancers is substantial — price it into your rates before choosing a country).
  • EU coordination rules and A1 certificates prevent double contributions when you work across EU states; the US has totalisation agreements with many EU countries so you don't lose pension credit.
  • Never go uninsured. Private international health cover bridges the gap until your local public registration is active.

Getting paid across borders without losing 10%

  1. Contract first, work second. Scope, rate, currency, payment terms (Net 14/30), revision limits and a termination clause — even a two-page template beats a handshake.
  2. Deposit from new clients: 30–50% upfront is normal and filters out non-payers.
  3. Multi-currency accounts (Wise, Revolut, Payoneer) usually beat bank exchange rates; Stripe or PayPal work well for smaller clients and productised services.
  4. Invoice properly: your legal name/business, client details, invoice number, date, itemised services, tax treatment (e.g. reverse charge), payment details.
  5. Watch the overpayment scam: a "client" overpays by card, asks you to refund the difference — then the original payment bounces. Any client who wants money to flow backwards before work starts is a scam warning sign.

Finding your first (and next) clients

  • Marketplaces: Upwork, Fiverr and Toptal (tech), Malt (France/Benelux), Freelancer.com — fast start, platform fees, race-to-the-bottom pricing at the low end.
  • Direct and referral work pays 2–5× marketplace rates: polish your LinkedIn, publish case studies, tell your network you're available, and ask every happy client for one introduction.

Expat freelancer setup checklist

  1. Confirm your current visa's work conditions — before taking the first client.
  2. Choose the right permit for your situation (freelance visa, digital nomad visa, or salaried work + side gig where legal).
  3. Register locally: tax number, VAT if needed, health insurance, business licence.
  4. Set up the money rails: local account + multi-currency account + invoicing tool.
  5. Price with tax, insurance and dead months included — your gross is not your income.
  6. Book one session with a local tax advisor in your first month.

FAQ — freelancing as an expat

  • Can I freelance on a tourist visa?
    No. A tourist or short-stay visa does not allow any productive work — including remote work for clients abroad. Working while on a visit visa can get you fined, banned and refused future visas. Use a dedicated freelance, self-employment or digital-nomad permit instead.
  • Which European countries have a freelance or digital nomad visa?
    Germany has a well-established freelance permit for liberal professions; the Netherlands runs a points-based self-employment route (and the DAFT treaty route for US citizens); Spain and Portugal offer digital-nomad visas for remote workers with steady income; Estonia has a digital nomad visa plus e-Residency for running an EU company online; Czechia issues a trade licence (živno) popular with freelancers.
  • Do I pay tax in my home country or where I live?
    Usually where you are tax resident — and staying more than about 183 days in a country typically makes you resident there. Double-taxation treaties stop you paying twice, but the details depend on your nationality and destination. US citizens must file US returns regardless of where they live.
  • How do I get paid by clients while living abroad?
    Invoice with a proper contract and agreed currency, use a multi-currency account (Wise, Revolut, Payoneer) or a payment processor (Stripe, PayPal) for smaller clients, and take a deposit of 30–50% from new clients. Keep every invoice for tax and visa-renewal purposes — income proof is often required to extend your permit.
  • Is freelancing on the WintWorks job board possible?
    The WintWorks board lists jobs from employers and some contract roles — every listing links to the original source so you can see the terms. For steady freelance pipelines, also use the major platforms plus your own network, and always verify clients before working for free or paying anything upfront.
