How to Get a Work Visa in Europe: EU Work Permit Guide (2026)

Reviewed by WintWorks Editorial Team · Last updated August 31, 2026 · 10 min read

Looking for a Europe work visa or an EU work permit? Whether you call it a work visa in the EU or a European work permit, there is no single visa for the whole continent. The country where you will work normally decides the permit, the salary threshold, the documents and the application process.

This guide gives you the shortest path to the right route, explains the difference between a work permit and a Schengen visa, and links to live Europe jobs and country-specific guidance.

Quick answer

Choose a country, match your profile to its work route, secure a qualifying job offer or prove eligible remote income, then apply through the official authority. A tourist or short-stay Schengen visa is not a work permit.

Is there a Schengen work permit?

No — there is no Schengen work permit. Schengen is a border-free travel area, while the right to work comes from national immigration law. A short-stay Schengen visa may allow permitted travel or business activities, but it normally does not authorise you to take a local job.

  • If you will work in Germany, apply under the German work and residence route.
  • If you will work in France, apply under the French route.
  • If you will work in the Netherlands, apply under the Dutch route.

Your nationality, role, salary, qualifications and intended length of stay determine the route. Always verify the current rule with the destination country's immigration authority or embassy before paying a fee.

Important: the UK and Switzerland are in Europe but are not EU work-permit systems. A permit issued by one country does not automatically authorise employment in another.

Route 1 — The EU Blue Card

  • Who: highly qualified non-EU workers with a qualifying job offer and salary
  • Where: most EU countries, with country-specific rules and exceptions
  • Perks: family-reunification options and mobility under qualifying conditions
  • Check first: the destination country's salary threshold, degree or experience rules and application process

Route 2 — National employer-sponsored permits

  • Germany — Skilled Immigration routes: options vary by qualification and occupation; start with jobs in Germany
  • Netherlands — Highly Skilled Migrant: the employer generally needs recognised-sponsor status; see jobs in the Netherlands
  • Ireland — Critical Skills: a route for eligible occupations and offers; see jobs in Ireland
  • UK — Skilled Worker: requires a licensed sponsor and an eligible role; see UK visa sponsorship jobs

Route 3 — Digital nomad and remote-work residence

Some European countries offer a residence route for people working remotely for a foreign employer or clients. These routes are not a substitute for a permit to take a local job.

  • Portugal and Spain: national remote-work routes with their own income, insurance and client rules; see Portugal and Spain.
  • Croatia, Estonia, Greece, Romania and Cyprus: each has separate eligibility and income requirements.

Route 4 — Job-seeker routes

  • Germany: the Opportunity Card is a time-limited route for eligible applicants to look for work under specified conditions.
  • Portugal: a job-seeker visa may allow eligible applicants to search before changing to the appropriate work-residence status.
  • Netherlands: the orientation year is mainly for eligible recent graduates and is not a general work visa.

Route 5 — Intra-company transfers and study-to-work

  • ICT permit: transfer within a multinational company to a European branch, subject to the country's rules.
  • Study to work: some countries give eligible international graduates time to find a job after study; check the national conditions.

How to apply for a European work visa: 5 steps

  1. Check eligibility. Compare your nationality, qualification, occupation, salary, language level and intended stay with the destination country's requirements. For a detailed country-by-country breakdown, read Detailed EU work permit and visa routes.
  2. Choose a country and find a qualifying offer. Browse live Europe jobs on WintWorks, or start with Germany, the Netherlands, the UK, Spain, France, Poland, Portugal, Ireland, Italy, Switzerland or Austria.
  3. Confirm sponsorship. Ask whether the employer can sponsor the exact role and whether the offer meets the current permit threshold. State your work-authorisation status early.
  4. Prepare the documents. Typical documents include a passport, employment contract, qualification evidence, proof of funds or accommodation, insurance and translations where required.
  5. Apply and register. Apply through the official immigration portal, embassy or consulate. After approval, complete the destination country's address, tax, social-security and residence registration steps.

📌 Avoid work visa scams

No legitimate visa process requires you to pay a recruiter for a guaranteed job. Government fees are paid through official channels. Learn how to check a job or visa offer.

FAQ — Europe work visa and EU work permit

What is a Europe work visa?

There is no single Europe work visa. The country where you will work normally issues a national work and residence permit. Common routes include an EU Blue Card, a national skilled-worker permit, an employer-sponsored permit, a job-seeker route or a digital nomad residence permit for qualifying remote workers.

How do I get a work visa in Europe?

Choose the country and route that match your situation, find a qualifying job or prove remote income where required, collect the documents, and apply through the relevant government portal, embassy or consulate. The employer often starts or supports an employer-sponsored work permit.

Is there a Schengen work permit?

No. Schengen is a travel area, not a work-authorisation system. A short-stay Schengen visa normally does not allow you to take a local job. You need the work and residence permit issued by the country where you will be employed.

What is an EU work permit?

An EU work permit is permission to work in a specific EU country. It is usually issued under that country's national rules. The EU Blue Card is a specialised work-and-residence route for qualifying highly skilled non-EU workers; it is not a permit to work in every EU country automatically.

Which European country has the easiest work visa?

There is no easiest country for everyone. Germany, the Netherlands and Ireland have established sponsored routes for many skilled roles, while Portugal, Spain, Croatia and other countries may suit eligible remote workers. Compare the route, salary, occupation, language and nationality requirements before choosing.

Does an EU work visa let me work anywhere in Europe?

No. A work permit is normally tied to the issuing country, employer and role. Even an EU Blue Card does not give an automatic right to start a job in another country; moving usually requires a new application or a qualifying mobility procedure.
