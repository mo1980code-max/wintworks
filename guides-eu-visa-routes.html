European Work Visas 2026: Every Route Explained

Reviewed by WintWorks Editorial Team · Last updated August 25, 2026 · 18 min read

If you want to work in Europe and are not an EU/EEA citizen, the first question is not which country — it is which route. Europe does not have a single work visa. Each country runs its own system, its own salary thresholds, its own processing times and its own list of eligible occupations.

This guide explains the main routes available across Europe in 2026, what they cost, who qualifies, and where to check official information before you apply.

The five main routes to work in Europe

Almost every European work permit falls into one of these five categories. Knowing which one fits your situation saves time and money.

EU Blue Card — the most portable skilled-worker route

The EU Blue Card is a work and residence permit for highly qualified non-EU nationals. It was created by EU Directive 2009/50/EC and updated by Directive 2021/1883 (the "Blue Card Directive recast"), which took effect in 2023 and whose implementation deadline for member states was December 2023.

What the 2021 recast changed

  • Lower salary thresholds: The general threshold dropped to 1.5 times the average gross salary; for shortage occupations (IT, maths, science, engineering, health) the threshold is 1.2 times.
  • Shorter experience requirement: Two years of professional experience replaces three years for some cases.
  • More flexible after arrival: After two years (rather than 18 months–3 years depending on country), Blue Card holders can change employer more easily and move to another EU Blue Card country under simplified conditions.
  • Faster processing: Member states must decide within 90 days.
  • Family reunification: Spouses can work immediately; family reunification is generally faster.

Which countries participate

The EU Blue Card is available in most EU member states, but not all. Countries that do not issue EU Blue Cards include Denmark and Ireland (they run their own points-based or national systems). Some countries issue it under different names or with different rules.

Essential: always check the national implementation, because the directive sets the framework and each country fills in the details.

General EU Blue Card requirements

  1. A job offer or employment contract for at least the minimum salary threshold set by the country.
  2. A university degree (or equivalent professional qualification in some countries).
  3. For shortage occupations, the lower salary threshold (1.2× average) often applies.
  4. Valid travel documents and proof of accommodation.
  5. Health insurance or proof of access to the national health system.

Processing time varies by country but is capped at 90 days under the directive for the initial decision.

Portability

One of the Blue Card's biggest advantages: after holding it for a set period, you can move to another Blue Card country under simplified rules — typically with a shorter processing time and reduced documentation.

National work permits — country-by-country

When the EU Blue Card does not fit — because your role is not highly paid, you do not have a degree, or the country does not offer the Blue Card — you apply through the country's national system.

Below is a summary of the main national permit types by country. Always verify the current rules on the official government website before applying, because thresholds and rules change regularly.

🇩🇪 Germany

EU Blue Card — for degree-holders with a salary above the threshold (generally €45,300 for shortage roles in 2024; adjusted annually).

Skilled Worker Visa — for people with a recognised vocational qualification or degree and a job offer. Following the 2024 immigration law reform, this route is broader than before.

Opportunity Card (Chancenkarte) — a points-based job-seeker visa launched in June 2024. Points are awarded for qualification, language skills, experience, age and connection to Germany. Valid for up to one year to look for work or complete an trial period.

IT Specialist Visa — for IT professionals without a formal degree but with proven experience (at least 3 years) and a salary of at least €51,840 (2024 figure, subject to change).

🇬🇧 United Kingdom

Skilled Worker Visa — the main route for most foreign workers. Requires a job offer from a Home Office-licensed sponsor, a salary at or above the going rate for the occupation (and generally at least £38,700 for most new applicants from 2024, with some exceptions), and English proficiency.

Health and Care Worker Visa — a cheaper, faster route for eligible health professionals with a sponsor. Lower fee and no immigration health surcharge in some cases.

High Potential Individual (HPI) Visa — for graduates of a top global university (listed by the Home Office) within the last 5 years. No sponsor required, but limited duration and no direct path to settlement on this route alone.

Global Talent Visa — for leaders or potential leaders in science, arts, culture or digital technology. Endorsed by a recognized body.

→ Browse UK visa sponsorship jobs · Official: GOV.UK Skilled Worker

🇳🇱 Netherlands

Highly Skilled Migrant (HSM) — for professionals earning above the threshold (€5,331/month for 30+ year-olds, €4,237/month for under-30s; 2025 figures, update annually). Employers with a recognised sponsor status get faster processing (often 2 weeks).

Skilled Labour in Shortage (Netherlands) — for roles on the official shortage list, with a lower salary threshold and sometimes a shorter procedure.

Highly Educated Migrant — for degree-holders with a job offer; separate scheme with its own salary threshold.

→ Browse jobs in the Netherlands · Official: IND recognised sponsors

🇫🇷 France

EU Blue Card (France) — for degree-holders with a salary at least 1.5× the average (or 1.2× for shortage occupations and certain roles).

Talent Passport (Passeport Talent) — a set of sub-categories for skilled workers, investors, artists, and people with an exceptional talent. Some sub-categories do not require a prior job offer.

Employee Card (Salarié) — the standard work permit for employees with a job offer and a labour market test (or exemption).

Intra-Company Transfer — for employees of a multinational transferred to a French entity.

→ Browse jobs in France · Official: French Interior Ministry

🇪🇸 Spain

EU Blue Card (Spain) — for highly qualified workers with a degree and salary threshold met.

Work and Residence Permit for Workers (Cuentaajustada) — for employees with a job offer; the employer usually initiates the process.

Highly Qualified Professional — a simplified route for graduates and professionals with a job offer; fast-track in some cases.

Digital Nomad Visa (2023 onward) — for remote workers and freelancers serving non-Spanish clients; income threshold applies; leads to residence and (after 5 years) permanent residency.

→ Browse jobs in Spain · Official: Spanish Ministry of Foreign Affairs

🇮🇹 Italy

EU Blue Card (Italy) — for degree-holders with a salary at least 1.5× the average gross salary (or 1.2× for shortage occupations).

Subordinate Work Permit (Lavoro Subordinato) — the standard work permit; employer-sponsored; subject to annual Decreto Flussi quotas for non-EU nationals.

Self-Employment / Entrepreneurship — for freelancers and business founders; requires a viable project and often a visa request under the "dispensation from quota" for certain categories.

EU Blue Card + job mobility — after 2 years, Blue Card holders can change employer under simplified rules.

→ Browse jobs in Italy · Official: Visto Per Italiae (MARE)

🇵🇹 Portugal

Work Permit (Subordination) — employer-initiated; granted after a job offer and, in some cases, a labour market test.

Highly Qualified Activity — for professionals with higher education and a qualifying job; faster processing.

Digital Nomad Visa (D8) — for remote workers and freelancers; income threshold applies; leads to residence.

Job Seeker Visa — for certain nationalities; allows 120 days (extendable) to look for work or a company to hire you.

D7 (Passive Income) — for people with substantial passive income; not a work permit but often used by remote workers and retirees.

→ Browse jobs in Portugal · Official: SEF (Serviço de Estrangeiros e Fronteiras)

🇦🇹 Austria

EU Blue Card (Austria) — for degree-holders with a salary above the threshold; one of the more accessible Blue Card implementations.

Red-White-Red Card — a points-based system for skilled workers, graduates, start-up founders and other categories. Points awarded for qualification, language, age, work experience, and Austria-specific factors.

Skilled Worker Scheme (including seasonal) — for workers in shortage occupations; some routes do not require a degree.

→ Browse jobs in Austria · Official: Austrian Migration Office

🇨🇭 Switzerland

L Permit (Residence — Short-term) — for non-EU/EFTA workers under the annual quotas; tied to a specific employer; valid up to 1 year (renewable).

B Permit (Residence) — for non-EU/EFTA workers with a firm job offer; quota-based for third-country nationals; leads to C permit after 5–10 years.

EU/EFTA Freedom of Movement — for citizens of EU/EFTA states (not the same as the Blue Card).

Intra-Company Transfer — under the bilateral agreements; some categories easier than the general quota route.

Note: Switzerland controls non-EU immigration tightly via annual quotas (by sector and country).

→ Browse jobs in Switzerland · Official: State Secretariat for Migration (SEM)

🇮🇪 Ireland

Ireland is not in the EU Blue Card system. Its main route is the Employment Permit system, run by the Department of Enterprise, Trade and Employment.

General Employment Permit — for occupations on the Critical Skills or ineligible list; salary threshold applies; labour market test may apply.

Critical Skills Employment Permit — for roles on the Critical Skills list (tech, engineering, health, etc.); faster path to Stamp 4 (full work rights) after 2 years.

Intra-Company Transfer — for employees of multinationals transferred to Ireland.

→ Browse jobs (Ireland info also on UK page context) · Official: Enterprise Ireland Employment Permits

Digital Nomad Visas — which countries offer them?

Digital nomad visas are for people who work remotely — for a company based outside the host country, or as freelancers serving clients outside the host country. The rules differ, but they share a common pattern: proof of remote income, a minimum income threshold, and often a requirement that you do not work for local companies.

🇪🇸 Spain

Visa: Digital Nomad Visa (Law 20/2023, from 2023).

Income threshold: Generally around 200% of the Spanish minimum wage (as of 2024, ~€2,400/month; verify current figures). Remote workers and freelancers serving non-Spanish clients.

Outcome: First a residence permit; after 5 years, permanent residency.

Official (Spanish Ministry of Foreign Affairs)

🇵🇹 Portugal

Visa: D8 Digital Nomad Visa (introduced 2022, updated since).

Income threshold: 4× the Portuguese minimum wage (as of 2024, ~€3,280/month; verify annually). Remote workers and service providers for clients outside Portugal.

Outcome: Temporary residence; path to permanent residency and citizenship after 5 years.

Official: SEF

🇭🇷 Croatia

Visa: Digital Nomad Residency Permit (since 2021).

Income threshold: ~€2,260/month (2024, verify current). Remote workers and freelancers; stay up to 1 year (non-renewable as nomad, but leads to other options).

Official: Croatian Ministry of Interior

🇬🇷 Greece

Visa: Digital Nomad Visa (2021 onward).

Income threshold: €3,500/month (as of 2021, verify current). Remote workers serving clients outside Greece; family members can join.

Official: Greek Ministry of Foreign Affairs

🇲🇹 Malta

Visa: Nomad Residence Permit (2023 onward).

Income threshold: €2,700/month net (as of 2023, verify current). Remote workers and freelancers; family members can join in some cases.

Official: Residence Malta Agency

🇨🇾 Cyprus

Visa: Digital Nomad Visa (2023 onward).

Income threshold: €3,500/month (as of 2023, verify current). Remote workers and freelancers; family members can join.

Official: Cypriot government

🇪🇪 Estonia

Visa: Digital Nomad Visa (since 2020).

Income threshold: ~€4,500/month (average over 6 months, verify current). Remote workers and freelancers; stay up to 1 year (non-renewable as nomad).

Official: Estonian Police and Border Guard Board

🇱🇻 Latvia

Visa: Digital Nomad Visa (since 2023).

Income threshold: €2,815/month (2023, verify current). Remote workers and freelancers; stay up to 1 year.

Official: Latvian Migration Office

🇳🇴 Norway (optional)

Norway does not have a dedicated "digital nomad visa" but offers the Freelancer/Enterpreneur Visa and other permits that may apply depending on circumstances.

Official: Norwegian Directorate of Immigration (UDI)

Important: Digital nomad visas are designed for remote workers earning outside the host country. Most do not allow you to work for a local company or take a local job without switching to a proper work permit. Read the exact permitted activities before you apply.

Intra-Company Transfer (ICT) — moving within a multinational

If you already work for a multinational company with a European branch, the intra-company transfer route is often the fastest and most predictable path to a European residence permit.

In the EU, ICT is governed by Directive 2014/66/EU, which each country implements nationally. In the UK, there is an Intra-Company Visa (part of the points-based system).

What you typically need

  • You have been employed by the company for a minimum period (often 6–12 months for junior roles; longer for senior roles).
  • The company has a genuine European entity that wants to transfer you.
  • The role is managerial, specialist, or trainee (the exact categories vary by country).
  • Salary and working conditions meet local or company standards.

Advantages

  • Often faster processing than a standard work permit.
  • The company already knows the process (in many cases).
  • Can be a stepping stone to a national work permit later in some countries.
  • Usually does not require a labour market test, because you are not hired from the local labour market.

Limitations

  • Tied to the company and the specific entity — you cannot switch employers freely on this route in most cases.
  • Some countries limit the maximum duration for the trainee or junior category.
  • May not count toward permanent residency in all countries (check national rules).

The job-seeker route — when you have no job offer yet

Most European countries require a job offer before you get a work permit. A few offer a job-seeker or residence-to-search route, which lets you enter the country and look for work within a limited time.

Examples (verify current rules)

  • Germany — Opportunity Card (Chancenkarte): Points-based, launched June 2024. Up to 1 year to look for work or complete a trial employment. Requires basic German or English, qualification or experience, and enough financial means.
  • Portugal — Job Seeker Visa: For certain nationalities; allows 120 days (extendable) to find work or be hired.
  • Some other countries: Have limited job-seeker or "job search" provisions embedded in other permit types — check the national rules.

The job-seeker route is useful when you have strong qualifications but no offer yet, and you are willing to move first and search on the ground. It is not a work permit itself — you still need to get a proper work permit once you find a qualifying job.

Common mistakes to avoid

  1. Assuming "EU visa" exists. There is no single EU work visa. Each country has its own permit, its own thresholds and its own process. The EU Blue Card is a framework — but national rules are what you actually apply under.
  2. Using the salary threshold from a blog post. Thresholds change every year in most countries. Verify on the official government website on the date you apply.
  3. Applying for the wrong permit type. A digital nomad visa is not a work permit for a local job. A Blue Card with a low salary will be refused. Always match the permit to your real situation.
  4. Ignoring the labour market test. Some permits require the employer to prove no suitable EU/EEA candidate was available. This is an employer-side process in many cases — ask your prospective employer whether they have done it or can do it.
  5. Assuming recognition of your qualification is automatic. Some countries require your degree or vocational qualification to be officially recognised (e.g., through ZabIn Germany, ENIC-NARIC in many countries, or a specific recognition procedure). Start this early — it can take weeks or months.
  6. Not checking the sponsor register (UK). For UK visas, the employer must be on the Home Office register of licensed sponsors. If they are not, the visa cannot be issued under that route.
  7. Ignoring processing times. Some permits take days (e.g., Netherlands HSM with a recognised sponsor), others take months. Plan your relocation timeline realistically.

Frequently asked questions

Q: Can I move to Europe without a job offer?

Not usually for a work permit. A few countries offer job-seeker visas (e.g., Germany's Opportunity Card, Portugal's job seeker visa), but these are for looking for work, not for working immediately. You still need a proper work permit once you find a qualifying job.

Q: Is the EU Blue Card better than a national permit?

It depends. The Blue Card is generally more portable across EU countries and often has a clearer path to long-term residence. But it requires a degree and a salary above the threshold. A national permit may be the only option for roles below the threshold, roles without a degree, or countries that do not issue Blue Cards (like the UK and Ireland).

Q: Can I switch from a digital nomad visa to a work permit later?

Sometimes, but not automatically. Rules vary by country. In some cases you can switch permit categories from within the country; in others you must leave and reapply. Check the specific rules before relying on a later switch.

Q: Do I need to speak the local language?

For most work permits, no — there is usually no strict language requirement for the permit itself (though some routes, like the German Opportunity Card, award points for language skills). However, language skills help enormously in finding a job and settling in. Some permits or integration requirements may involve language over time (e.g., integration courses in Germany).

Q: Can my family come with me?

In most cases, yes — family reunification is available for many work permit holders, though the conditions (income threshold, housing, relationship proof) vary by country. For EU Blue Card holders, family reunification is usually faster and spouses can often work immediately. Check the national rules.

Q: How long does a work permit application take?

It varies widely: Netherlands HSM can be processed in about 2 weeks for recognised sponsors; UK Skilled Worker typically a few weeks; Germany Blue Card within 90 days (by law); other countries may take longer. Always check the typical processing time on the official website and apply well before you plan to move.
